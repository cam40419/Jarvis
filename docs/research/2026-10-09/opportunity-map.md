# Simon opportunity map

Original brainstorm: **October 9, 2026**. Owner decision: **October 10, 2026**.
The owner promoted five bundles into **required base platform quality**: accurate change
impact and bounded repair; native editable delivery packets; effective human review;
reliable completion at honest total cost; and authorized continuity. The
[platform baseline quality plan](../../architecture/platform-baseline-quality-plan.md)
defines their implementation and acceptance before business tools are accepted one at a
time. They remain required when a proposed mechanism fails; redesign the mechanism and
keep the acceptance gate open.

Simon can become more valuable by reducing the work between an intended business result
and an accepted, usable result. The opportunities below cover product experience, output
quality, execution, collaboration, privacy, economics, distribution and longer-term
research. The five promoted bundles are baseline scope; other mechanisms and commercial
packages remain design candidates rather than a commitment to implement every feature.

The October 9 commercial hypothesis favored small creative and product teams handling
research, documents, design assets and code across applications. The October 10 decision
retains Simon's general platform ambition. The clothing-brand pilot and other demanding
workflows provide measurement fixtures rather than limiting core product scope. Commercial
options are compared in the [strategy report](strategy.md). Product facts and competitive
threats retain their dates in the four market research notes.

For supported workflows, prespecify baseline acceptance against strong configured platforms
with the same goal and a competent existing stack, including cost and privacy conditions.
Baseline parity is required; 30% less owner effort is an optional research stretch target.
Tests below can reject an implementation or optional hypothesis, but cannot discard one
of the five requirements. New innovation beyond the baseline is identified separately in
the longer-term research and experiment priorities.

## Intake and explicit requirements

1. **A result contract.** Before work starts, show the deliverable, intended use, editable
   format, quality checks, permitted actions, budget and decisions the owner must make.
2. **A contradiction inbox.** Present incompatible source claims together, including their
   dates and authority, so a draft never silently becomes operating policy.
3. **A missing evidence plan.** Separate facts that can be retrieved from choices only the
   owner can make; ask questions that would change the next action.
4. **An archive completeness check.** Show imported, parsed, unsupported and unread files,
   including why an item was omitted from the working context.
5. **Progressive setup.** Let a first small, reversible task establish useful context before
   asking the owner to configure a whole virtual organization.

Test with ambiguous archives and a first-time owner. Reject the design if setup takes
longer than a brief and a shared folder, or if the owner cannot identify unresolved choices.

## Planning and coordinated project work

6. **Deliverable dependencies.** Link tasks to the exact inputs and accepted outputs they
   need, instead of relying on a predecessor card marked Done.
7. **Critical-path decisions.** Explain which missing decision blocks the outcome, its cost
   of delay, and the work that can proceed independently.
8. **Alternative plans.** Offer a small number of plans with different cost, quality and
   deadline tradeoffs, including assumptions that make each plan viable.
9. **A capacity-aware team.** Add a specialist only when necessary; expose the expected
   benefit, permission needs, cost and coordination overhead.
10. **A working project manager.** Escalate exceptions, consolidate duplicate work and
    maintain dependencies while leaving ordinary progress quiet.

Compare against a competent human using Asana or ClickUp plus a strong assistant. Extra
roles and a larger graph are failures when they increase coordination without improving
accepted work.

## Evidence and changes to accepted work

11. **A claim-to-source graph.** Attach material assertions, calculations and specifications
    to the exact source passages and versions that support them.
12. **Change impact.** When a requirement or source changes, identify the affected parts of
    accepted deliverables and propose bounded repairs.
13. **Policy and preference provenance.** Distinguish owner-approved rules from suggestions,
    observed habits and model-generated assumptions.
14. **Freshness obligations.** Give volatile facts an expiry or recheck condition appropriate
    to their use; preserve the historical evidence behind the old decision.
15. **Evidence-aware retrieval.** Explain relevant omissions, contradictions and permission
    boundaries rather than presenting a citation list as proof of complete coverage.

Test seeded source changes, irrelevant edits and revoked access. A useful system catches
material impacts without making every small change restart the whole project.

## Editable deliverables and format fidelity

16. **A source and preview bundle.** Keep editable source, rendered preview, validation
    results and accepted version together.
17. **Office validation.** Check workbook formulas and units, document pagination, slide
    overflow and native editability in the intended application.
18. **Structured creative specifications.** Store dimensions, color space, typography,
    placement, resolution and channel constraints as checkable data.
19. **A delivery manifest.** Explain which files are final, their intended use, dependencies,
    licenses, outstanding checks and the application required to edit them.
20. **A working-file guarantee.** Verify that delivered source opens and can be changed,
    rather than treating a good screenshot as sufficient evidence.

Editable export is already offered by many products, and some exports sacrifice native
objects to preserve appearance. Test actual files after export and reopening. [Genspark
Slides export limitations](https://www.genspark.ai/docs/ai_slides_faq).

## Human review and limited attention

21. **An evidence-first decision inbox.** Show the decision, changed material, blocking
    findings, cost and consequences before activity logs.
22. **Review only what changed.** Direct attention to material differences and show which
    earlier checks still apply.
23. **Risk-tiered review.** Use deterministic checks, source verification, independent model
    review and human expertise where each adds measured value.
24. **Coherent batch review.** Let an owner review a campaign or milestone together while
    preserving individual asset decisions and exact version bindings.
25. **A review workload forecast.** Budget human attention along with money and deadline;
    postpone low-value work when the owner's review queue is already overloaded.

Measure active review time, missed defects, unnecessary rejections and correction cycles.
Version comparison and creative approvals already exist in specialist proofing products;
the test is whether Simon makes the full production loop easier. [Ziflow review
workflow](https://academy.ziflow.com/hc/en-us/articles/35520118848660-Session-5-Reviewing-Proofs-in-Ziflow).

## Creative production and human direction

26. **Separate concepts from production.** Approve direction before expensive rendering or
    technical preparation; preserve rejected alternatives and their reasons.
27. **A reusable creative grammar.** Turn accepted style decisions into scoped, editable
    constraints without flattening the brand into a single prompt.
28. **Cross-asset consistency checks.** Review a campaign's language, visual hierarchy,
    claims and product details across its files.
29. **Production-readiness checks.** Validate supported print, placement, packaging and
    channel requirements; retain technical and physical review where needed.
30. **Human contribution records.** Preserve authorship, source-art restrictions and actual
    edits, with explicit permission for learning from work.

Begin with one accepted workflow and real editable files. A concept image is not proof of
manufacturing readiness, copyright clearance or a production-capable design adapter.

## Tools and external effects

31. **An action rehearsal.** Show a bounded plan, expected object changes and required
    permissions before enabling a new write workflow.
32. **A delivery-to-action binding.** Bind the approved asset, destination, account and action
    parameters together so changing any material part requires the applicable new decision.
33. **A verified operation catalog.** Publish only tested operations, account requirements,
    limits, recovery behavior and validation evidence.
34. **A recovery workbench.** Distinguish a known failure from a lost response after a possible
    external effect; show how to inspect, reconcile and continue.
35. **Compensating actions.** Offer an undo only when the external system supports it;
    otherwise describe a specific repair and its remaining consequences.

Compare with configured approvals and receipts in automation platforms. Do not promise
universal exactly-once effects or universal rollback.

## Privacy and ownership

36. **Visible data destinations.** Explain which data classes leave the machine, project,
    tenant or region for each enabled capability.
37. **Portable work records.** Export meaningful tasks, accepted files, decisions, evidence,
    costs and unresolved work in a documented format.
38. **Permission-aware memory.** Recheck the source authority when recalling knowledge;
    distinguish historical existence from current permission to disclose it.
39. **A private worker profile.** Support useful local execution with honest hardware,
    quality, setup and maintenance requirements.
40. **Customer-controlled retention.** Apply deletion and retention to originals, extracts,
    derived memories, previews and caches with visible exceptions.

Test cross-client work and access removal. Local inference alone does not establish that
all project data stays local.

## Learning from corrections

41. **Proposed lessons.** Suggest a rule from repeated owner corrections, with evidence,
    scope, expiry and an explicit way to reject it.
42. **Replay before applying a lesson.** Test a proposed rule on prior examples and held-out
    work before it changes future output.
43. **Negative examples.** Preserve why a candidate failed, not just the accepted result.
44. **A preference conflict check.** Surface conflicts between a client preference, project
    requirement and company policy.
45. **Opt-in shared learning.** Share reusable validators and procedures without silently
    training on confidential customer work or exporting client preferences.

Compare with existing procedural memory and reflection systems. A lesson that overfits,
leaks across clients or increases correction work should be rolled back.

## Model and resource economics

46. **Cost per accepted deliverable.** Show successful, rejected and repaired attempts,
    including tool costs and unresolved liabilities where available.
47. **Calibrated routing.** Choose models from measured task results, permitted data
    destinations and capacity, rather than model size alone.
48. **A review budget.** Spend on additional review only when the expected defect reduction
    justifies it; retain mandatory safety and authority gates.
49. **A bounded completion offer.** Quote a clearly scoped result with exclusions and a
    spending ceiling; stop and renegotiate when assumptions change.
50. **Honest free and local profiles.** Explain quota, hardware and unsupported capability
    limits without silently moving to paid or hosted inference.

Separate customer pricing from execution cost. Charging only for an accepted outcome can
create adverse incentives or unbounded provider liability; test pricing before committing.

## Reliability and recovery

51. **Recovery drills.** Exercise dispatch loss, duplicate callbacks, worker death and restore
    against every accepted connector operation.
52. **Business-state continuity.** Resume the project with its accepted decisions and
    unresolved obligations, not merely a transcript.
53. **Confidence in the record.** Show which outcomes were observed externally, which were
    inferred and which remain unknown.
54. **Restoration rehearsals.** Restore a quarantined project, verify artifact hashes and
    avoid replaying effects already performed externally.
55. **Bounded degradation.** Continue safe independent work when a provider or tool is
    unavailable; expose the blocked dependency and preserve the cost boundary.

Compare with documented recovery in agent runtimes and durable workflow systems. Reliability
is a testable requirement; it is not automatically a unique selling point.

## Collaboration and handoff

56. **Shared human and agent responsibility.** Keep human assignments, agent attempts,
    review work and blocked decisions visible in one project.
57. **Client review without full onboarding.** Give an external reviewer only the necessary
    material and decisions, with bounded links and appropriate access.
58. **Cross-company handoffs.** Pass an accepted package to a supplier or collaborator
    without exporting unrelated client context or credentials.
59. **Vendor-agent participation.** Use strong external coding, research or design agents
    as specialists while preserving one project authority.
60. **A human takeover packet.** Explain current state, remaining work, exact files,
    unresolved risks and the next safe action when automation cannot proceed.

Test handoff between two people and two runtimes. Portability that requires a specialist
to reconstruct the project may have little customer value.

## Interaction and usability

61. **Outcome-first navigation.** Organize the workspace around deliverables and decisions
    while keeping agent identities and logs available for inspection.
62. **Direct steering.** Let the owner edit a requirement, annotate an artifact or reassign
    work without rewriting a long conversational prompt.
63. **Quiet progress.** Reserve interruptions for choices that change the result or
    authority; provide a concise on-demand status.
64. **Accessible review.** Support keyboard review, clear focus, understandable states,
    readable comparisons and useful mobile decisions.
65. **Multilingual operating briefs.** Preserve technical terms, units and client style
    while testing actual multilingual output quality.

Measure successful owner tasks and comprehension, not only whether a browser scenario
passes. A readable record is insufficient if the user cannot steer it.

## Workflow packages and entry markets

66. **Creative launch package.** Support a small launch from evidence and brief through
    editable assets, source/code handoff and a publication proposal.
67. **Agency client workspace.** Keep several clients' preferences, permissions, deadlines
    and approval histories separate while reusing approved procedures.
68. **Research-to-decision package.** Deliver a claim map, alternative options, sourced
    analysis and a decision record with explicit unresolved questions.
69. **Internal operations package.** Automate one repeatable document or reporting workflow
    with measured error rates and a responsible owner.
70. **Technical creator package.** Coordinate code, procedural graphics, documentation and
    editable marketing assets for small product teams.

Choose named operators and demanding repeated jobs for measurable fixtures while preserving
the general core. Commercial packages and specialized verticals require their own domain
validation and distribution strategy.

## Ecosystem and integrations

71. **Accepted workflow packs.** Bundle a brief, procedure, tool versions, validation rubric,
    permissions, failure cases and honest support scope.
72. **Validator extensions.** Let domain experts add checks without granting the validator
    production write permissions.
73. **A connector acceptance badge.** Publish dated results for named operations and account
    types, with regression tests and an accountable maintainer.
74. **Existing-stack participation.** Connect to a customer's board, file store or automation
    engine when that reduces migration burden; define one authority per object.
75. **Expert procedures as products.** Let specialists distribute reviewed workflows and
    acceptance criteria, with provenance and opt-in usage evidence.

Avoid a connector-count contest. Marketplace supply, maintenance and trust have to exist
before a marketplace interface adds value.

## Distribution and adoption

76. **An accepted first task.** Make the trial produce a small useful deliverable with its
    checks and handoff, rather than a tour of agent configuration.
77. **A before-and-after demonstration.** Show the same workflow in a conventional stack
    and Simon, including mistakes, repairs, time and total cost.
78. **Practitioner partnerships.** Develop workflow packages with designers, agencies and
    operational specialists who understand actual delivery standards.
79. **Adoption through exports.** Let accepted output enter the buyer's existing tools before
    asking the whole team to move their workflow.
80. **A public evaluation corpus.** Publish sanitized task definitions, failures and limits
    that an independent team can reproduce.

Interview target buyers and test repeat use before building a broad sales motion. Vendor
distribution and existing application habits are major competitive advantages.

## Commercial model and operations

81. **Clear platform and model charges.** Separate Simon's subscription, included capacity,
    third-party inference, tool costs and optional customer keys.
82. **Project-level cost reports.** Allocate charges to an understandable deliverable or
    workflow instead of opaque credit movement.
83. **Shared project allowances.** Support a team budget with visible reservations and
    deliberate overage policy.
84. **A paid concierge experiment.** Measure whether a buyer will pay for repeated accepted
    work before promising a fully self-service version.
85. **Supportable reliability tiers.** Offer service commitments only after measuring the
    deployment, recovery procedure and cost to support it.

A subscription must cover hosting, support and unsuccessful work as well as model costs.
Do not price from a best-case model demonstration.

## Evaluation and defensible evidence

86. **Held-out defect sets.** Test unsupported claims, wrong units, stale sources, malformed
    files and subtle visual defects that the maker has not seen.
87. **Repeated trials.** Measure consistency, variation in cost and worst-case review effort,
    rather than reporting one successful run.
88. **Fair strong baselines.** Compare the best configured rival and a competent person
    using a normal stack, including setup and custom maintenance.
89. **A complete outcome record.** Preserve inputs, configurations, attempts, accepted files,
    human effort and unresolved charges with the evaluation result.
90. **Independent review calibration.** Measure false acceptance and unnecessary rework for
    each review method, including model agreement and blind human review.

Generic benchmarks provide methods and component checks; a Simon buyer's actual workflow
needs its own acceptance rubric. [WorkArena](https://github.com/ServiceNow/WorkArena),
[tau2-bench](https://github.com/sierra-research/tau2-bench).

## Longer term research

91. **Project branches.** Explore competing directions in isolated work branches, compare
    accepted outputs and merge a chosen direction while preserving rejected alternatives.
92. **Business-state simulation.** Rehearse a plan against a bounded model of inventory,
    capacity, approvals and external effects; label what the simulation cannot predict.
93. **Reproducible cross-tool work.** Re-run a supported production process with pinned code,
    assets, fonts, templates and parameters, then compare actual outputs.
94. **Verified procedure compilation.** Translate an outcome contract into typed operations
    and review gates whose invariants can be checked independently of the model.
95. **A learning operating model.** Use accepted outcomes and failures to improve planning
    without silently changing a client's rules or granting new authority.

These candidates have high integration and validation costs. Prototype them against an
actual repeated workflow before treating them as a platform advantage.

## Alternative product strategies

96. **A portable acceptance layer.** Let existing agents do the work while Simon records
    requirements, exact deliverables, acceptance, repairs and current obligations.
97. **A private production companion.** Serve teams whose important assets and licensed
    applications live on their own machines.
98. **A specialist workflow service.** Sell a narrowly defined accepted outcome with human
    expertise, using Simon as the operating platform behind it.
99. **A developer platform.** Expose the work/acceptance contracts as APIs for other products
    after proving that external developers need them.
100.  **A reusable core with a narrow product.** Keep general contracts inside Simon while
      marketing one clearly successful customer workflow.

The October 9 proposal favored the last commercial option, with selective portable
acceptance and private production. The October 10 decision keeps the general platform
and its five baseline requirements; a narrow workflow is a verification fixture, not an
approved narrow-product or service pivot. Alternative packaging remains a hypothesis.
Broad control-plane or model-provider entries carry separate delivery obligations.

## Required baseline acceptance and separate future experiments

BASE-01..05 below follow the canonical quality plan; BASE-06 supplies their comparative
gate. All required outcomes belong to core acceptance. Reference/generic operations and
declared native imports, manifests and validators prove the base contracts before each
business tool is accepted and inherits them. Failed required tests block acceptance and
trigger repair or redesign.

This is the initial brainstorming pass across twenty themes. Three independent iterations
challenge buyer assumptions, technical costs and workflow value:
[product and distribution](challenge-product-and-distribution.md),
[systems and economics](challenge-systems-and-economics.md), and
[workflow and quality](challenge-workflow-and-quality.md). They add narrower mechanisms and
commercial routes, sometimes overlapping the catalog, and reject generic review/board
positioning. The [final strategy](strategy.md) records changes and experiment gates.

Remaining avenues stay tied to an experiment or customer question. A finite brainstorm
cannot exhaust every conceivable idea; coverage here is material themes and alternatives,
rather than an unbounded implementation backlog.
